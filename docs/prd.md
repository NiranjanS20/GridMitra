# 1. Executive Summary

## 1.1 Product

**Mohalla Grid** is a neighbourhood-scale flexibility operating system
that helps a local feeder absorb renewable intermittency and grid stress
without waiting for major grid reinforcement.

It combines:

1.  **Forecast** --- predict feeder load and local PV availability.
2.  **Prioritize** --- classify demand into critical,
    essential-business, and comfort tiers.
3.  **Shift** --- move flexible consumption into periods of renewable
    surplus or lower feeder stress.
4.  **Store** --- dispatch shared community battery capacity using
    transparent reserve and fairness rules.
5.  **Report** --- provide the DISCOM with feeder-stress forecasts,
    voltage alerts, and verified demand-response results.

The official challenge describes a software-led layer serving roughly
**100--300 households and shops** at an LT
feeder/distribution-transformer level. It specifically asks teams to
demonstrate measurable reliability improvement, local manageability,
affordability, DISCOM usefulness, architecture, simulation, and an
ownership/economic model.

## 1.2 Core product proposition

> **When renewable output falls or feeder supply becomes constrained,
> Mohalla Grid predicts the gap early, protects critical loads, shifts
> flexible demand, dispatches shared storage, and proves the resulting
> reliability improvement electrically.**

## 1.3 Prototype principle

The prototype is not a physical microgrid. It is a **digital twin +
forecasting + optimization + control + dashboard system**.

The prototype should be capable of taking a synthetic but calibrated
neighbourhood feeder, replaying a day/week of load and PV conditions,
injecting grid constraints or outages, and comparing:

-   **Baseline:** unmanaged feeder + normal battery/backup behaviour.
-   **Mohalla Grid:** forecasting + uncertainty + demand response +
    shared battery dispatch + priority protection.

# 3. Problem Statement

## 3.1 Primary problem

A neighbourhood connected to a constrained distribution feeder can
experience reliability problems when:

-   renewable generation changes faster than expected,
-   evening demand rises after solar output falls,
-   flexible loads remain uncoordinated,
-   battery capacity is poorly scheduled,
-   operators lack sufficient feeder-level visibility,
-   critical and non-critical loads are treated identically,
-   forecast uncertainty is ignored,
-   grid constraints such as voltage violations are discovered after
    dispatch rather than before it.

## 3.2 User pain points

### Residents

-   Power interruptions affect refrigeration, study, work and home
    businesses.
-   Individual backup such as an inverter or diesel generator can be
    expensive relative to household income.
-   Residents may not know when to shift flexible consumption.
-   They need simple communication rather than an engineering dashboard.

### Small businesses

-   Outages can interrupt refrigeration, payment systems, machinery and
    working hours.
-   Businesses may accept demand-response events if the benefit is
    transparent.

### Local operator / Urja Mitra

-   Needs one operational view of battery state, feeder stress, forecast
    risk and active events.
-   Needs safe automation rather than raw AI commands.
-   Needs clear reasons for each dispatch decision.

### RWA/cooperative

-   Needs transparency on battery usage, credits, costs, maintenance and
    fairness.
-   Needs an auditable ownership and revenue model.

### DISCOM

-   Needs feeder stress visibility.
-   Needs verified rather than self-reported demand-response.
-   Needs voltage and loading information.
-   Needs a system that can operate without major main-grid
    intervention.

# 4. Product Vision

## Vision

**Make local electricity flexibility programmable, measurable and
accessible at neighbourhood scale.**

Mohalla Grid does not attempt to replace the DISCOM or rebuild the
distribution network. It adds a local intelligence layer between
variable supply, flexible demand, shared storage and the utility.

## Product principles

1.  **Reliability before optimization profit.**
2.  **Safety before AI autonomy.**
3.  **Forecast components before forecasting one opaque gap variable.**
4.  **Every optimization decision must be explainable.**
5.  **Local operation must continue during cloud loss.**
6.  **Fairness must be explicit and auditable.**
7.  **No invented performance claims.**
8.  **No dependency on proprietary hardware in the prototype.**
9.  **Every data source must carry lineage and caveats.**
10. **Power-flow simulation validates electrical feasibility; ML does
    not replace the power-flow solver.**

# 7. Goals, Success Criteria & Non-Goals

## 7.1 Product goals

1.  Demonstrate measurable reliability improvement against a baseline.
2.  Demonstrate feeder-level operation with minimal central
    intervention.
3.  Protect critical loads during constrained supply.
4.  Reduce evening feeder stress through storage and demand response.
5.  Quantify forecast uncertainty rather than only producing point
    predictions.
6.  Verify that dispatch actions are electrically feasible.
7.  Provide a transparent fairness mechanism.
8.  Provide useful, auditable information to a DISCOM.
9.  Produce a defensible economic model without inventing prices.

## 7.2 Success criteria

The prototype is successful if it can:

-   replay a complete representative day and week;
-   forecast load and PV;
-   produce uncertainty intervals;
-   generate correlated gap scenarios;
-   calculate an optimization schedule;
-   enforce battery and priority constraints;
-   reject unsafe commands;
-   simulate baseline and solution conditions;
-   quantify outage hours and demand served;
-   quantify critical-load availability;
-   quantify feeder evening peak;
-   quantify voltage violations;
-   calculate verified DR;
-   display decisions and explanations in dashboards.

**No numerical improvement target is hard-coded until the simulation
produces it.**

## 7.3 Explicit non-goals

The hackathon prototype will not:

-   directly control a live electrical feeder;
-   replace protection relays;
-   replace DISCOM SCADA/ADMS;
-   claim regulatory approval;
-   claim islanding safety from software alone;
-   claim production-grade cybersecurity certification;
-   claim that a GNN replaces pandapower or physical power flow;
-   claim 15-minute PV forecasting superiority without suitable data;
-   invent household electricity prices;
-   require blockchain;
-   require physical battery hardware for the prototype.

# 9. End-to-End Product Journey

## 9.1 11:00 --- Solar surplus

1.  PV generation exceeds current local flexible demand.
2.  Forecast engine detects continued surplus.
3.  Optimizer charges the shared battery within SOC limits.
4.  Flexible loads receive an opportunity signal.
5.  Resident credits are calculated from verified response.
6.  DISCOM receives a surplus/flexibility status.

## 9.2 16:00 --- Shortfall predicted

1.  Weather/irradiance inputs indicate possible cloud reduction.
2.  Load model predicts evening demand rise.
3.  PV model predicts generation decline.
4.  Gap scenarios are generated.
5.  Probability of shortage crosses the configured event threshold.
6.  Reserve requirement increases according to uncertainty.
7.  Residents receive a message such as:

> "High evening demand is expected. Run your water pump between
> 4:00--5:00 PM and earn flexibility credits."

## 9.3 19:00 --- Peak

1.  Feeder demand reaches its evening peak.
2.  Battery dispatch starts according to the optimized schedule.
3.  Critical loads remain protected.
4.  Flexible loads are shifted/curtailed according to opt-in rules.
5.  Voltage is checked using the feeder model.
6.  The safety checker validates all commands.
7.  DISCOM sees the verified kW/kWh response.

## 9.4 Night --- Constrained supply

If the simulated grid is constrained:

1.  Critical loads remain highest priority.
2.  Essential business loads are maintained according to available
    energy.
3.  Comfort loads are curtailed first.
4.  Battery reserve is protected.
5.  The operator is alerted if reserve is projected to become
    insufficient.

# 10. Functional Requirements

## 10.1 Forecasting

### FR-001 --- Load data ingestion

**Priority:** P0

The system shall ingest timestamped feeder/household load data and
associated calendar/weather features.

**Acceptance criteria** - 15-minute records are accepted. - Missing
intervals are flagged. - Duplicate timestamps are rejected. - Time zone
is stored explicitly. - Data lineage is recorded.

### FR-002 --- PV data ingestion

**Priority:** P0

The system shall ingest PV generation and/or irradiance observations.

**Acceptance criteria** - PV generation is represented in kW. -
Irradiance inputs are timestamp-aligned. - Missing PV observations are
flagged. - Physical PV capacity is stored as metadata.

### FR-003 --- Smart persistence baseline

**Priority:** P0

The system shall implement clear-sky-index persistence.

**Definition**

\[ CSI_t = `\frac{P^{PV}_t}{P^{clear}_t}`{=tex} \]

and:

\[ `\hat{P}`{=tex}\^{PV}*{t+h} = CSI_t `\times `{=tex}P\^{clear}*{t+h}
\]

where the clear-sky PV curve is generated using a physical model.

### FR-004 --- Seasonal naive baseline

**Priority:** P0

The system shall compare against same-time previous-day and
previous-week baselines where data supports them.

### FR-005 --- Quantile load forecast

**Priority:** P0

LightGBM shall produce lower, median and upper quantile forecasts.

Candidate features:

-   lagged load;
-   rolling mean/std;
-   hour;
-   day of week;
-   holiday flag;
-   temperature;
-   humidity;
-   irradiance;
-   previous-day load;
-   previous-week load.

### FR-006 --- Quantile PV forecast

**Priority:** P0

The PV model shall use clear-sky-normalized generation plus
satellite/weather features.

The preferred formulation is:

\[ P\^{PV}\_t = P\^{clear}\_t `\times `{=tex}CSI_t \]

The ML model forecasts CSI or its bounded transformation rather than
relearning the deterministic day/night shape.

### FR-007 --- Uncertainty calibration

**Priority:** P0

The system shall implement conformalized quantile regression.

Metrics:

-   empirical coverage;
-   interval width;
-   pinball loss;
-   coverage by hour;
-   coverage during cloudy events.

### FR-008 --- Adaptive uncertainty

**Priority:** P1

Adaptive conformal inference shall update nonconformity thresholds as
recent forecast error changes.

### FR-009 --- Scenario generation

**Priority:** P0

The system shall generate joint load/PV scenarios using correlated
residual sampling.

Recommended approach:

1.  obtain load residuals;
2.  obtain PV residuals;
3.  transform residuals to rank/uniform space;
4.  estimate dependence;
5.  sample correlated residual pairs;
6.  reconstruct load/PV trajectories;
7.  calculate gap scenarios.

A Gaussian copula is the initial implementation. Equivalent dependence
models may be substituted if documented.

### FR-010 --- Walk-forward validation

**Priority:** P0

No random train/test split shall be used for the time-series forecasting
evaluation.

Required pattern:

-   chronological training;
-   validation on future periods;
-   test on later unseen periods;
-   configurable gap to prevent temporal leakage.

The verification document recommends a starting configuration of
`TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=5, gap=96)` for 15-minute data; this is a
tuning starting point, not a universal requirement.

### FR-011 --- Leakage prevention

**Priority:** P0

If the deployed system would use a weather forecast, the
training/evaluation pipeline must use archived forecast information
available at the forecast issuance time.

Observed future weather shall not be silently substituted for forecast
weather.

# 12. Renewable Gap

## 12.1 Definition

For each time interval:

\[ G_t = L_t - P\^{PV}\_t - P\^{grid,available}\_t \]

where:

-   (L_t) = feeder demand;
-   (P\^{PV}\_t) = available local PV;
-   (P\^{grid,available}\_t) = available grid supply under the simulated
    grid condition.

For normal grid-connected operation, the grid availability term may be
large enough that the gap is negative. For a grid-constrained or
islanded condition, the term becomes constrained.

The optimization uses the **distribution of future gap scenarios**,
rather than relying on a single point estimate.

## 12.2 Reserve

Reserve should increase when:

-   forecast uncertainty increases;
-   cloud variability increases;
-   expected evening peak increases;
-   grid availability becomes uncertain;
-   participation in DR is uncertain.

A conceptual reserve rule is:

\[ R_t = R\_{base} + k `\cdot `{=tex}`\sigma`{=tex}(G_t) \]

The coefficient (k) must be tuned through simulation rather than
asserted as a universal value.

# 13. Optimization & Control

## 13.1 M5 --- Scenario-based MPC/MILP

The optimizer operates over a rolling horizon.

Decision variables may include:

-   battery charging power;
-   battery discharging power;
-   battery SOC;
-   flexible-load shift;
-   load curtailment;
-   grid import;
-   PV curtailment;
-   reserve allocation.

## 13.2 Objective

A weighted objective can minimize:

\[ J = w_1 C\_{grid} + w_2 C\_{peak} + w_3 C\_{degradation} + w_4
C\_{unserved} + w_5 C\_{curtailment} + w_6 C\_{fairness} \]

Reliability penalties must dominate economic optimization when critical
loads are threatened.

## 13.3 Battery constraints

For every timestep:

\[ SOC\_{t+1} = SOC_t + `\eta`{=tex}\_c P\^c_t `\Delta `{=tex}t -
`\frac{P^d_t \Delta t}{\eta_d}`{=tex} \]

subject to:

\[ SOC\_{min} `\le `{=tex}SOC_t `\le `{=tex}SOC\_{max} \]

\[ 0 `\le `{=tex}P\^c_t `\le `{=tex}P\^c\_{max} \]

\[ 0 `\le `{=tex}P\^d_t `\le `{=tex}P\^d\_{max} \]

Additional constraints:

-   no simultaneous charging and discharging unless explicitly supported
    by the solver formulation;
-   reserve requirement;
-   battery availability;
-   degradation cost;
-   maximum cycle/ramp constraints where applicable.

## 13.4 Load tiers

### Tier 1 --- Critical

Examples:

-   refrigeration for food/medicines;
-   medical equipment;
-   critical communications;
-   designated community safety loads.

### Tier 2 --- Essential business/community

Examples:

-   small-shop refrigeration;
-   payment terminals;
-   water pumps;
-   selected productive loads.

### Tier 3 --- Comfort/flexible

Examples:

-   geysers;
-   discretionary EV/e-rickshaw charging;
-   selected cooling loads;
-   flexible appliances.

Tier classification is configurable and must be visible to the
resident/operator.

## 13.5 Fairness rules

The system shall implement:

-   need-weighted priority;
-   per-household access caps;
-   anti-hoarding limits;
-   transparent event participation;
-   credit accounting;
-   configurable protected minimum service;
-   audit logs.

Fairness is not allowed to be an invisible side effect of the optimizer.

## 13.6 Demand response

DR event states:

1.  Proposed
2.  Notified
3.  Accepted/opted-in
4.  Active
5.  Measured
6.  Verified
7.  Credited
8.  Closed

Verified DR:

\[ DR\_{verified} = Baseline\_{estimated} - Load\_{actual} \]

Only verified response contributes to the DISCOM settlement metric.

# 14. Safety Layer

The control architecture must be:

**Forecast → Optimization → Safety Checker → Command**

Never:

**Forecast → Direct command**

The safety checker must verify:

-   SOC limits;
-   battery power limits;
-   reserve requirement;
-   feeder thermal constraints;
-   voltage constraints;
-   phase imbalance limits where modeled;
-   critical-load protection;
-   valid device state;
-   command freshness;
-   operator authorization.

Any failed check produces:

-   command rejection;
-   reason code;
-   safe fallback action;
-   audit log.

# 15. Cloud Loss & Forecast Failure

## 15.1 Cloud loss

If cloud connectivity fails:

-   edge controller continues operating;
-   latest valid forecast remains available;
-   fallback rules take over;
-   new cloud commands are rejected until authenticated state is
    restored.

## 15.2 Forecast degradation

If forecast quality degrades:

1.  increase reserve;
2.  reduce aggressive arbitrage;
3.  prioritize reliability;
4.  use conservative battery schedule;
5.  trigger operator warning.

The system must be tested with forecasts degraded by 30%.

# 16. Grid Simulation & Electrical Validation

## 16.1 Purpose

The simulation demonstrates that software decisions are not only
mathematically feasible but also electrically plausible.

## 16.2 Two-level validation

### Level A --- Benchmark

Use a standard balanced benchmark such as pandapower's `case33bw` for
reproducibility and comparison.

Important caveat:

> The standard 33-bus benchmark is an MV benchmark, not an Indian 415 V
> LT feeder.

### Level B --- Target feeder

Create a custom 415 V three-phase LT network representing:

-   residential customers;
-   small businesses;
-   rooftop PV;
-   transformer;
-   shared battery;
-   phase imbalance;
-   flexible loads.

Use pandapower's unbalanced three-phase power-flow capability
(`runpp_3ph`) for voltage-violation claims.

## 16.3 Why unbalanced modelling matters

Indian LT networks can exhibit phase imbalance. A balanced model can
hide voltage problems that appear when single-phase loads and PV are
distributed unevenly.

## 16.4 Voltage violation

For each bus/phase:

\[ V\_{min} `\le `{=tex}V\_{i,`\phi`{=tex},t} `\le `{=tex}V\_{max} \]

The exact limits used in the prototype must be stated in the experiment
configuration and linked to the applicable modelling
assumption/standard.

## 16.5 GNN boundary

The GNN must **not** replace pandapower.

The GNN is used only for the proposed P2 use case:

> **Sparse-meter state estimation**

Observability ladder:

1.  Transformer meter only
2.  Transformer + selected smart meters
3.  Transformer + smart meters + PV inverter telemetry

The GNN estimates unobserved state from available measurements and
topology.

Electrical feasibility is still validated through the power-flow model.

# 19. Data Leakage Policy

Forecasting must simulate what would have been known at prediction time.

### Prohibited

Using future observed irradiance to predict PV generation at a time when
the system would only have had a weather forecast.

### Required

Where possible:

-   archive forecast issuance time;
-   store forecast horizon;
-   store forecast model/source;
-   train on forecast-available information;
-   compare against observed outcome.

# 28. Non-Functional Requirements

The local safety/fallback layer must not depend on cloud availability.

## NFR-002 --- Forecast latency

Prototype target: complete one forecast run within a few seconds for a
single feeder on a development machine.

This is an implementation target, not a field guarantee.

## NFR-003 --- Optimization latency

Prototype target: produce a dispatch schedule within the time required
for the demonstration loop.

## NFR-004 --- Explainability

Every automated action must have:

-   reason;
-   model/optimizer version;
-   forecast ID;
-   constraints;
-   expected impact.

## NFR-005 --- Auditability

Every write/action must have:

-   timestamp;
-   actor;
-   resource;
-   old/new state where applicable;
-   reason;
-   request/event ID.

## NFR-006 --- Privacy

Smart-meter data can be sensitive personal information. The production
design must assess applicability of India's data-protection requirements
and implement:

-   data minimization;
-   role-based access;
-   encryption;
-   retention policy;
-   consent/notice where required;
-   pseudonymous customer IDs for modelling.

## NFR-007 --- Offline degradation

If network connectivity is unavailable:

-   edge controller remains active;
-   cached schedule remains usable for a limited period;
-   safe fallback is activated;
-   cloud synchronization occurs after recovery.

## NFR-008 --- Observability

System logs must capture:

-   API latency;
-   forecast runs;
-   optimization failures;
-   rejected commands;
-   voltage violations;
-   battery reserve violations;
-   data quality issues.

## 31.3 Unit economics

Do not use invented prices.

### Capex

\[ CapEx = BatteryCost + PCS + Gateway + Metering + Installation +
CivilWorks \]

### Capex per household

\[ CapEx\_{HH} = `\frac{CapEx}{N_{HH}}`{=tex} \]

### Monthly cost

\[ Cost\_{monthly} = Amortization + O&M + OperatorCost + Insurance +
SoftwareCost \]

### Monthly revenue

\[ Revenue\_{monthly} = Subscriptions + VerifiedDR + ToDValue +
BusinessTier + OtherApprovedRevenue \]

### Break-even

\[ BreakEvenMonths = `\min `{=tex}t: `\sum`{=tex}*{i=1}\^{t} Revenue_i
`\ge`{=tex} CapEx + `\sum`{=tex}*{i=1}\^{t} OperatingCost_i \]

### Affordability

Compare:

\[ MonthlyFee\_{MohallaGrid} \]

against the target customer's existing backup expenditure, including
inverter/diesel/other relevant backup costs where data is available.

# 43. Appendix A --- Model Selection Rationale

## Why LightGBM instead of deep learning as the default?

The verification research recommends LightGBM as the core because the
expected training data is relatively small/tabular, it is
computationally inexpensive, interpretable, and practical for edge
deployment.

Deep temporal models should be treated as ablations rather than
assumptions of superiority.

## Why clear-sky normalization?

Solar output has a large deterministic component caused by sun position
and time of day. Normalizing against a clear-sky physical curve focuses
the learning problem on cloud/intermittency behaviour.

## Why conformal prediction?

A point forecast does not tell the optimizer how much reserve is
required. Calibrated prediction intervals provide a way to translate
uncertainty into reserve decisions.

## Why scenario generation?

Load and PV forecast errors can be correlated. Treating the renewable
gap as a single independent random variable can misrepresent joint
uncertainty.

## Why scenario MPC/MILP?

Battery SOC, power limits, reserve, priority, DR and fairness are hard
constraints. A constrained optimizer is easier to audit than a black-box
reinforcement-learning controller.

## Why pandapower?

The optimizer may produce a mathematically valid schedule that creates
voltage problems. Power-flow simulation provides an electrical
feasibility check.

## Why GNN only as stretch?

The verification document specifically concludes that a GNN is not
justified as a replacement for physical power flow. Its more defensible
role is state estimation under sparse metering.

# 46. Final Product Definition

Mohalla Grid is **not simply an AI forecasting application**.

It is a complete neighbourhood flexibility system:

``` text
              ┌──────────────────────┐
              │   WEATHER + LOAD     │
              │   + PV OBSERVATIONS  │
              └──────────┬───────────┘
                         ↓
              ┌──────────────────────┐
              │      FORECAST        │
              │ Load + PV + Uncertainty
              └──────────┬───────────┘
                         ↓
              ┌──────────────────────┐
              │   GAP SCENARIOS      │
              │ Joint uncertainty    │
              └──────────┬───────────┘
                         ↓
              ┌──────────────────────┐
              │   OPTIMIZER          │
              │ Battery + DR + Reserve
              └──────────┬───────────┘
                         ↓
              ┌──────────────────────┐
              │   SAFETY CHECKER     │
              │ Electrical + battery │
              │ + priority constraints
              └──────────┬───────────┘
                         ↓
              ┌──────────────────────┐
              │  FEEDER SIMULATION   │
              │ pandapower / LT grid │
              └──────────┬───────────┘
                         ↓
              ┌──────────────────────┐
              │  MEASURED OUTCOME    │
              │ Reliability + cost + │
              │ voltage + fairness   │
              └──────────────────────┘
```

The product's core differentiator is therefore the **closed decision
loop**:

> **Predict → quantify uncertainty → decide → safety-check → validate
> electrically → measure outcome → learn.**

That loop, combined with feeder-level operation, transparent
priority/fairness rules, resident-friendly communication, and verified
DISCOM-facing demand response, is the central implementation thesis of
this PRD.
